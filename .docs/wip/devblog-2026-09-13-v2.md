### The horde runs. Here's the Sunday it started.

It's the 12th of October and I've got the horde demo up on the big monitor. One hundred thousand agents on a 5120×1440 screen, and the frame counter reads 58.[^1] [unverified: 58 is the integrated-loop figure from the 2026-09-07 M6 benchmark (100,000 agents, 17.32 ms p50); the 83 the ledger gave was M4's render-only number, and later work has changed the frame, so read the live counter.] Frames a second, a frame being one finished picture of everything on screen. An agent here is one enemy, one row in a table, nothing more.

I pull the camera all the way back and the horde stops being a crowd and turns into ants.

Each one's tinted by which tier it's in, so you can read the brains from the color. The ring near the hero gets the full treatment: looks at its neighbors, shoves, steers. The middle band gets one push-out pass.

The far ones get nothing. They drift along a flow field, a precomputed map of which way to lean, and they cost almost nothing, which is the whole reason there can be a hundred thousand of them.

I press F3. The on-screen readout shows the progress of a ten-second profile capture, and when it lands it shows the file name. There's a named command that summarizes it, so I never have to remember the flags. I told the AI assistant "I won't recall the cli and args" and the flags went away.

#### The second demo is the one that's over budget

Same chair, second window. Twenty thousand Ybots walking. Ybot is the stock Mixamo character, the grey robot every prototype uses [unverified: the sources say only that this is the Ybot from the O3DE Multiplayer Sample, Mixamo's character; "grey" and "every prototype" are not in them], and these ones were cooked into the game's content folder with a 22-bone rig. Cooked is the project's word for turning source art into the files the runtime actually reads.

Twenty-two isn't a number anybody measured first. Stock Mixamo is 65 bones, and the Ybot mesh itself only uses 52 of them. I said "the easy pickins are we drop down to a baked mesh for the hand in a loose grip and remove all the finger bones," 52 minus 30 finger bones is 22, and when the budget finally got measured it came out around 26 bones at 20,000 agents. I'd guessed inside it blind.

And it's over budget. Twenty thousand animated agents in one integrated frame measured 20.93 milliseconds at the median, against a budget of 16.67, that's one sixtieth of a second.[^4] That's 4.26 milliseconds over, and it stays on the screen with the rest. I'm not hiding it behind the ants.

Then the renderer. Five hundred point lights, the kind that throw light in every direction from one spot, lit across the full 5120×1440 frame in 2.07 milliseconds.[^2]

Then the thing that doesn't look like anything: one call to set a property on a test scene node, sent three ways. As a direct Python call, over MCP, which is how an AI tool talks to a program, and over gRPC, the network kind. The result comes back byte-identical each time.[^3] Any agent, human or machine, would drive the engine through the same door.

All of it is Python. All of it was one week in September, the 6th to the 13th.

#### Why nothing's moved in a month

SpriteJammer has sat mostly still since the middle of September, and it isn't because it stalled. [unverified: the source set ends 2026-09-13, so nothing in it shows what happened after; the owner's quote below is the only evidence] The week I'm about to tell you about made me want to build something else first. Here's how I put it this morning: "that work inspired me to do other work - and I want that work done before I keep making SpriteJammer awesome ... that's how important the whole thing was."

What made the week work wasn't the code. It was the pile of documents the machine gets loaded with every morning before it touches anything: a handoff file that says what the last session knew and I'd otherwise lose, a journal written while the work happened instead of after, and every estimate written down before the measurement, so the measurement had something to catch.

None of that needs an engine. It needs a folder, and the habit of writing things down before the machine can make you forget them. Write the guess down before you measure, so the measurement has something to catch.

#### Sunday, 17:08, and I asked for the wrong game

Five weeks ago, on Sunday the 6th of September, I opened an empty folder and typed one paragraph at a coding assistant. "You are a very experienced technical director and 3D rendering specialist working in video game development. This folder is for a new project, I haven't commited anything yet to github. I want to build a 2.5 D + HDR game engine and level editor for a metroidvania style game..." Then a stack: wgpu, a modern graphics layer, for the drawing; Python for as much as it could stand; PySide6 and Dear ImGui for the editor windows; a list of physics libraries I hadn't picked between; and three rendering schemes with no decision. "I am hoping forward+ but am willing to explore Gbuffer(s), hybrid or deffered rendering." Typos and all.

I told it to think it through and report back.

Nineteen minutes later I'd changed the game. At 17:27 I wrote that the feeling I was after was "the hundred man march" from StarCraft 2, that moment a wall of units comes over the ridge at you [unverified: the quote says only "a large horde of enemy soldiers streaming at me"]. "So a sub-set of Starcraft2 meets Smash TV."

A metroidvania has fifty enemies on screen. This has ten thousand. The entity model the assistant had just recommended, the plan for how the game keeps track of every enemy, was dead on arrival, and that's fine.

Same message, two paragraphs up: "Let's start a /docs folder and keep a historical record, knowledge base, managed context - I am going all in with AI-first development." I'd also called it a personal research project, right there, in the first half hour. The plan would forget that part within two days. I'd have to say it again.

#### VS Code ate the session twice, so I wrote down what I'd lose

By 18:01 the window had reloaded and the session was gone. A session is one continuous conversation with the assistant, and everything it's worked out lives only there. Something about editing the workspace file had made VS Code reload the project, and VS Code couldn't see the conversation that had been running since 17:08 anymore. I typed "feel free to own and modify the .code-workspace file. something got interrupted, resume" and kept going.

By 18:38 it'd happened again.

This time I typed "resume" and kept typing: "something about editing the workspace caused vscode to reload the project and it couldn't see this session anymore - on that signal alone, let's write a comprehensive /docs/handoffs/ document so if we do loose this session and context we can rehydrate a new one that isn't missing out on anything important." That document's still there, and every session starts by reading it.

The first standing instruction written into it: don't touch the workspace file. The user owns it.

That's the handoff system, born from being annoyed twice in forty minutes. It carries the things you can't get back by reading the code: what I prefer, what I've already said no to, which decisions I drove against the assistant's recommendation and why.

#### Every estimate was wrong in the same direction

Later that night the first spike finished. A spike here is a time-boxed experiment that exists to answer exactly one question and then gets frozen as evidence, and this one asked how long one simulation step, one tick, of ten thousand agents takes on the CPU in Python.

Three estimates were already on the record. The architecture notes the assistant had written that afternoon said 7 to 15 milliseconds. An outside review I'd commissioned that same day, a second AI handed the whole plan with instructions not to be diplomatic, said 2 to 5. The assistant disputed the review and recounted it at 3 to 7.

Then it ran. 19.6 milliseconds.[^5] Every guess was low, and 93% of the 19.6 was one line of code, the part where each agent looks for its neighbors... the exact line the review had called the only defensible one.

I didn't tell it how to fix that. What I said wasn't a fix, it was closer to a shrug: "'Proof of life' for 10k agents is important … but them doing much probably isn't."

Ten thousand bodies on screen matter. Ten thousand brains don't.

That clause became Behavior LOD. LOD is level of detail, the old graphics trick of spending less on whatever's far away, and here it's applied to the brains instead of the drawing. It's the tiering you saw in the ants. Near agents get the full neighbor gather and steering, mid agents get one push-out, far agents ride the flow field. The expensive part now runs on about fifteen hundred agents instead of ten thousand.

With that and Numba, a compiler that takes the hot Python loops and turns them into machine code, the tick fell to 1.97 milliseconds.[^6] Under every estimate, including the wrong ones. The architecture flipped on it the same night: both tiers of the horde live on the CPU now, and the GPU is an escape hatch above thirty thousand, not the design.

#### The metric said 2.5×. The picture said blob.

Same night, a different spike, and this time the guesses ran high. Impostors are flat pictures of a character baked from a ring of angles so the renderer can draw a card instead of a mesh, and the question was how much texture memory ten characters with three animations each would eat. The outside review had said about 600 megabytes. The assistant had guessed 40. It measured 25.

Then the assistant tried a "utilization fix": fit the character to its tile in X and Y independently so less of each tile was wasted. The fill metric went from 11% to 29%.[^7] That's 2.5×, and it was about to go in the report as a win.

Somebody opened the PNG first. The character was a squat, stretched blob. The metric had improved precisely because the shape was wrong.

Reverted.

At 21:43 I asked for something that had nothing to do with rendering. "every time you journal, review the AI session logs associated with this project and try to tell a human story about me directing you - you discovering, pushging back, etc."

Until then the journal had been one file per topic, written up afterward. From that message on it's one file per session, appended while the session is happening, and the thing that most reliably earns an entry is a belief changing. The blob's in there. So is the 19.6.

#### "If it ever becomes fun we will talk"

Monday went to building. By the small hours of Tuesday there were six milestones done (a milestone being a numbered slice of work that ends in something you can run) and the seventh on the plan said it'd pass when the thing "feels like a game."

At 01:08 on the 8th I wrote: "just extrapolate going forward ... this is a research project. if it ever becomes fun we will talk otherwise." M7 was withdrawn within the hour. The plan had drifted from what I'd typed in the first thirty minutes, and the assistant had let it.

At 03:40 I wrote "I can't run m7" and got two answers. The literally correct one was that M7 was withdrawn. The useful one was that the whole renderer, the one that handles the lights, didn't exist outside a throwaway script you couldn't launch. `make s3` existed by the end of the day.

That's where the 500 lights come from.

#### Camera 4 feels better

At 15:29 I was torn about the camera, and I said so: "Starcraft2 is the best RTS and one of my favorite games, so let's make it feel like that and absorb the impact ... oblique might be the more interesting route though from a technical perspective and pipeline. I am torn."

The assistant didn't pick a side. It asked what each camera does to the edge of a 32:9 frame and turned taste into arithmetic. StarCraft 2's own 35° lens at 16:9 skews an object at the screen edge by 61% and draws it 1.27× bigger than at center, and an 18° long lens pulled back to 55 meters at 32:9 reproduces that almost exactly.[^8] The cameras went into the demo and I drove each one.

At 15:58: "Camera 4 feels better, so I guess we are making a 3D game ..."

A gate in this project is a decision only I can make, and nothing downstream of an open one gets built. That closed the gate. It'd been open since the first Sunday. The decision went into an ADR, an architecture decision record, which is append-only: you never edit the old reasoning, you write a new one that supersedes it.

#### Three days of red and a README that lied

On the 11th the assistant was reading the names of CI's checks for some unrelated reason. CI is the server that runs every test on every push, and it'd been red, failing, on `main`, the copy everything else is built from, since the 8th.[^9] Three days. Three sessions of commits, some with messages that said the gate was green.

Two test files imported the graphics library at load time. The CI machine has no graphics card and no library to go with it, so both files crashed before a single test could run, and the run executed no tests at all. The messages said green because on my machine, where the library's installed, it was.

Same day I noticed the README still said "Phase 0, pre-spike. No engine code exists yet." Six milestones in. I wrote "I just noticed the project readme is stale - process should gate updating this and part of DoD for significant increments of work - right?" and now the doc check fails the build unless the README links the newest benchmark, the newest written-up measurement.

Earlier that day I'd asked for the thing I should've asked for on day one. "We have enough complexity that process should start to favor not only branch development, but pull requests, as this gives us better review capabilities (human, and other AI like copilot), and a incremental history that proves valuable. agree? or push back?"

A pull request is a proposed change that sits in the open for review before it lands. It didn't push back. `main` got locked behind a ruleset and nothing's gone in without one since.

On the 12th, one more. Every commit the assistant made carried a trailer crediting itself as co-author. "I don't like the idea of AI by default attributing itself to the work. I am the director, designer, human in the loop taste, and game dev expert - ai are just machines operating on my intent." From then on the trailer's gone. The journal still says which session did what. That's the record, not credit.

#### Sunday again, and I asked for this post

The 13th of September was a Sunday. The three-way call passed that day.

The same day, in another session, I wrote: "I want a new weekly 'post dev blog to codex' skill or somtething that runs sunday afternoon."

And then what it should be: "I want to tell a human story ... and the first run should start with the very first prompt for this project, tell the story how it transitioned from a 2.5D metrodvaina to a horde and survior game - BUT most important, how this repo was setup ai-first, knowledge database, journaling, process, as that's what has made it an excellant research project."

The very first prompt was 17:08, one Sunday earlier, one paragraph into an empty folder.

That was a month ago. This is the first run.
