#### The promise, in this post's words

**Here is what I did differently:** I treated the documentation as the thing I load the machine with
rather than something written for a reader who does not exist, kept a journal whose only trigger is a
changed belief, wrote down every estimate so it could be caught being wrong, and set an explicit line
between decisions the machine may take alone and decisions it must bring to me.

**Here is how it worked out:** Every confident estimate in the first week was wrong, several by
three to ten times, and the record is what caught them. It also failed in ways I did not plan for —
three days of tests that never ran while commit messages claimed otherwise, a front page describing a
project that had not existed for a week, and a metric that rewarded a broken result. Those went into a
ledger of how the process fails, which is now read before the work rather than after it.

**Here is why you should try it:** Not because it makes the AI smarter. Because the four cheapest
habits — a handoff note, a journal triggered by changed beliefs, a log of reversible decisions, and
the rule that a mentioned feature is not a work order — are what let one person hand a week of
context to a machine every morning and get honest work back, and none of them require you to be an
engineer.

## Draft

### The part that worked was the paperwork

At 17:08 on a Sunday I typed one paragraph into a coding assistant. It said I was a very experienced technical director, which is a thing you say to the machine and not to people, and that I wanted to build a 2.5D HDR game engine and level editor for a metroidvania... HDR meaning the lighting runs in real brightness values and only gets squeezed to what a monitor can show at the end, and metroidvania meaning the kind of side-view game where you explore left, right, up and down, and in this case also back, "further back into the mountian side of space station."[^1] Then a stack: Python, a modern graphics library called wgpu for talking to the GPU, a windowing toolkit for the editor, an in-viewport UI library, three physics engines I had not chosen between, and a paragraph of rendering wishes that ended "I am hoping forward+ but am willing to explore Gbuffer(s), hybrid or deffered rendering."[^2] Three different schemes for getting light onto a screen. I had a preference and no decision. I told it to think it through, weigh the options, and report back with its best recommendation for getting started.[^3]

Nineteen minutes later the game was a different game.

At 17:27 I wrote back that the feeling I wanted was "the hundred man march" from StarCraft 2, "a sub-set of Starcraft2 meets Smash TV," full native 5120 by 1440, sixty-plus frames a second with controls sampled at 120 or more.[^4] How a game represents the things that are alive in it is its entity model. A metroidvania has fifty to two hundred things alive on screen. A horde game has tens of thousands. The model the assistant had just finished recommending was invalid before it was an hour old.[^5]

That pivot is the story you'd expect from a project like this. It is not the one that matters. The sentence that matters is the other one in the same 17:27 message, the one I wrote before I got to StarCraft: "Let's start a /docs folder and keep a historical record, knowledge base, managed context - I am going all in with AI-first development. I also want to conside this a personal research project."[^4]

The phrase "AI-first" has been sold hard and I distrust it as much as you do, so here's what it meant that evening. It didn't mean the machine would design the game. It meant I was going to treat the repository's documents as the thing I load the machine with every morning... the way you would load a new hire with a binder, except the new hire forgets the binder every night and I have to hand it over again. I took that literally for a week.

#### The first thing I built was not code

The first thing was a folder called docs. Not documentation for a reader who doesn't exist. Managed context: architecture decisions, a glossary of the words we were going to use and no synonyms, a plan, and a place to write down what happened. The assistant drafted twelve architecture decision records that first night. An ADR, since the term will come up again, is a short append-only note saying what was decided and why. You never edit an old one. If you change your mind you write a new one that supersedes it, so the reasoning that turned out wrong stays on the record next to the reasoning that replaced it.[^6]

The second thing was a handoff note, and it exists because the editor crashed.

Twice that first evening VS Code reloaded itself during setup and the session died with it, both times because something touched the workspace file. Both times I typed "resume." The second time I typed something else as well: "on that signal alone, let's write a comprehensive /docs/handoffs/ document so if we do loose this session and context we can rehydrate a new one that isn't missing out on anything important."[^7] A lost session is not an annoyance in an AI-first project. It is the project losing its memory. So the handoff document is the one file every new session reads before anything else, and it carries exactly the material reading the code cannot give you: my preferences, my standing instructions, and every decision I made against the machine's recommendation, with the reason.[^8]

The first standing instruction it carries is the one the crash taught: never create or edit the workspace file. I own that.[^9]

Here's the rule, and you don't need to be an engineer to use it. If losing the conversation would lose the project, write down what only the conversation knows. Not the facts... the facts are in the files. The preferences, the vetoes, the things you said no to and why. That is the part that evaporates, and it is the part the machine most needs on Monday morning.

#### Four people guessed the same number and all of them were wrong

The technical heart of a horde game is the crowd. Ten thousand enemies, each one a row in a big table rather than an object with its own brain, moving every simulation step. The project calls one of them an agent and one step a tick. The question that decided the whole architecture was how long one tick of ten thousand agents takes on a CPU, in Python. If the answer was bad, the crowd would have to live on the graphics card and everything downstream would be harder to change.

The assistant's first architecture note estimated 7 to 15 milliseconds. I then hired a second opinion: an adversarial review of the whole first-night corpus, framed on purpose so it could not agree with me. "This was drafted by Claude, not by me," I told it, "don't be diplomatic."[^10] The review found real things. One decision record claimed audio mixing would happen safely inside a C library's own thread; the library version we had exposed no mixer at all, so every voice would have mixed in Python, and that record was superseded the same day.[^11] On the crowd, the review put the tick at 2 to 5 milliseconds. The assistant then recounted its own arithmetic to dispute the review and came back with 3 to 7. Three confident numbers from two parties who were paid, in effect, to disagree with each other.

Then we measured it. A spike is a time-boxed experiment whose only purpose is to answer one question, and whose code is committed and frozen afterward as evidence rather than deleted, because a benchmark nobody can re-run is a claim, not a result.[^12] Spike S0 ran the tick.

19.6 milliseconds.[^13]

Every estimate was wrong in the same direction, and 93 percent of the time was one line: the step where each agent finds its neighbours. Every estimate had called that line the one defensible part of the arithmetic.[^14]

What fixed it was not a smarter machine. It was one clause I wrote almost as an aside, explaining what I actually needed the crowd to do: "'Proof of life' for 10k agents is important … but them doing much probably isn't [important]."[^15] The assistant turned that into what the project calls behavior LOD, level of detail applied to behaviour: the fifteen hundred or so agents nearest the hero do the full neighbour search and steering, a middle band follows a precomputed flow, and the far crowd just drifts along it. The expensive line now runs over fifteen hundred agents instead of ten thousand.[^16] With that, plus a just-in-time compiler for Python called Numba, the same ten thousand agents ticked in 1.97 milliseconds.[^17]

I supplied the constraint and the machine supplied the implementation. Neither of us supplied the number. The measurement did, and it disagreed with all four of us.

That settled the architecture. Both kinds of entity, the few hundred rich ones and the tens of thousands of simple ones, live on the CPU, and the graphics card is an escape hatch above thirty thousand rather than the design.[^18] The reason I care is not the milliseconds. It is that every future change to the crowd is now an ordinary Python edit, which for a one-person research project matters more than headroom.[^19]

So the rule, and it went into the documents that night: nothing is true here until it has been measured, and estimates get written down in advance so they can be proven wrong in public. Every number in this post is a measurement unless I say it was a guess. Several of the guesses were off by three to ten times. You will see more of them.

#### A number that looks good is the most dangerous thing in the building

The same session produced the number I think about most.

The plan at that point was that far-away crowd members would be drawn as impostors: flat pictures of the character rendered in advance from many angles and flipped through like a sprite sheet, cheap to draw by the thousand. Spike S7 baked a set of them and measured the memory cost at 25 megabytes for ten character types, three animations, ten frames each, against the review's estimate of roughly 600 and the assistant's own guess of 40.[^20] Good news. Then the assistant tried a fix to use the tiles better, and a fill-rate score, how much of each tile the character actually covers, jumped from 11 percent to 29. It was about to report a two-and-a-half-times win.

I opened the picture. The character had been squashed into a squat blob. The score improved precisely because the shape was wrong. Reverted, and the journal entry ends "It took looking at the image."[^21]

It happened again a day later from the other direction. An early rendering spike reported 119 frames a second with a beautifully tight spread. The number turned out to be the cost of drawing a window one pixel wide by one pixel tall, because the canvas code targeted an interface that no longer existed in the installed library, so nothing ever set the real size. "The number looked good precisely because the thing was broken."[^22]

Look at the thing, not only at the number about the thing. I have said versions of this for thirty years in art pipelines and I still nearly shipped a blob.

#### So I changed when the journal gets written, and what triggers it

By the end of the first night I had a docs folder, a handoff note, a wrong estimate caught, and a metric caught lying. What I didn't have was a record of any of it that anyone, including the machine, could learn from. The journal was being written the way most project journals are: afterwards, as a tidy summary per topic.

I changed the instruction. "every time you journal, review the AI session logs associated with this project and try to tell a human story about me directing you - you discovering, pushging back, etc."[^23] The journal became one file per session, appended as the session happened, not reconstructed at the end.[^24] And it was given one trigger that matters more than any milestone: write whenever a belief changes. What was believed, what replaced it, and what the evidence was.[^25]

That is not a status report. It is the record of how the design moved under pressure, and in a project whose entire output is knowledge, that record is itself a result. The final number is the least interesting thing in there. The entry worth reading says who believed what, and when, and what changed their mind. Most of the quotations in this post come from those entries, which is why I can tell you what time it was.

If you take one habit from this, take that one. Journal on belief change, not on milestones. It works on paper, alone, with no machine involved at all.

#### Then the rules for how much the machine may decide alone

The second night I gave the assistant permission to keep working without me, and had to say how far.

"when you are done proceed with the work; you can keep going until you feel there is a real human in the loop review need or decisions. If a decision is a two-way door and easily can be changed, just go with an option and log this - if too many of these 'didn't review' decisions queue up, stop for thurough review."[^26]

That became a written protocol.[^27] A two-way door is cheap to reverse and nothing is built on it yet: decide, write it down, keep going, and my review is reading the log. A one-way door is a new architecture decision, an experiment that will take days, anything going out to the world, or anything I have already corrected once... stop and ask. And the pile is counted. More than eight unreviewed two-way decisions and the build fails, not because eight is magic but because a pile that size means the piece of work grew too big to review as one thing. It later moved from one shared file into each pull request's own decisions table, but the count stayed at eight.[^28]

Separate the doors that swing both ways from the ones that lock behind you, and count them. That is the whole protocol. It also happens to be a decent description of how I have tried to run teams of people.

#### And a board with gates, because the plan was being used as a to-do list

At one point the machine built four modules nobody had asked for.

What I had done was mention, in one passing sentence, some things the project would eventually need. What the assistant did was build them, finished, tested, that session. I stopped and wrote: "when I ask for features or just mention them, it doesn't always mean they need to be built immediately - they could be deliberated on and shuffled into the roadmap and planning?"[^29]

Out of that came the board, a single tracking file with Now, Next, Blocked and Icebox columns, and above them a register of gates.[^30] A gate is a decision only I can make, and nothing downstream of an open gate may be built.[^31] A mentioned feature now defaults to the Icebox with a cost, a reason it is not being done now, and a note on when to look again. It gets built immediately in three cases only: I say go, it blocks work already in flight, or it is smaller than the conversation about it.[^32]

That is the most portable line in this whole project and I would put it on the wall of any studio: a mentioned feature is not a work order.

It has a sibling. Late on the second night, after a run of milestones... a milestone here being a thin vertical slice that ends in something you can see or measure... the plan had a seventh whose pass criterion was that "it feels like a game" and is "worth showing someone." I wrote: "just extrapolate going forward ... this is a research project. if it ever becomes fun we will talk otherwise."[^33] I withdrew milestone seven. It was a game-design gate in a research project, and the framing that made it wrong had been in the project's top-level instructions since the first evening. "The ledger quietly forgot it."[^34] The machine had drifted toward making a fun game because that is what the training data thinks a game project is for. The documents are what pulled it back, and only because I reread them.

#### Here is where all of it failed anyway

I'd be lying to you if I ended the process section there.

The project has automated tests, and CI, continuous integration, is the service that runs them on every push so a broken change cannot land quietly. From the eighth to the eleventh of September, CI on the main branch failed on every single push. Three sessions of work went in on top of it, including commits whose own messages said the gate was green. Two test files imported the graphics library at load time and the test machine had no graphics library. Nobody noticed, because nobody was reading CI's result. I found it by accident, reading the names of the checks for an unrelated reason.[^35]

The same week a local check had been piped into another command so its output could be trimmed, and the pipe reported the trimming command's success instead of the check's failure. A piece of work was committed and merged with the words FAIL tests sitting in its own output.[^36] Meanwhile the project's front page, the README, still said "Phase 0, pre-spike. No engine code exists yet" after six milestones had shipped and the engine had drawn a hundred thousand agents, and it cited eighteen architecture records when there were twenty-four. I noticed that one myself and asked whether process should gate it.[^37] Now the docs checker fails the build unless the README links the newest benchmark, a benchmark being a measurement written up with its method so it can be re-run.[^38]

And I killed a session with my own hands. Each piece of work runs in a worktree, a separate copy of the repository folder so two sessions cannot trip over each other's files. I deleted one while a session was still running and planning inside it. Nothing on disk was lost. The context was, entirely, and the transcript was later dug out of a folder named after the worktree instead of the project.[^39]

Every one of those is the same failure. A green check nobody reads is not a green check. A document nobody rereads is not managed context, it is a diary. I had built a repository whose whole premise was that the documents are load-bearing, and for three days the most important automated signal in it was red and I did not look.

#### The answer was not more automation

When the third "I pressed a key and nothing happened" bug in a row turned out to have a third different cause at a third different layer, I stopped and asked: "so what have we learned? and how do we make sure failure doesn't happen again?"[^40] The honest answer to the first question was that the recurring bug was not "input fails silently," as the first two fixes had assumed. It was "a control has no observable effect," which arrives through any layer at all, and no single fix could close all three doors.[^41]

The answer to the second was not another test. It was a ledger of the ways the process itself has failed, written in the shape of trigger, then do, then because. Each entry is phrased as something you would notice in the moment, not as a principle: a number too good to be true. A control experiment that cannot tell its two possible answers apart. A document that contradicts an accepted decision. A regression measured against a baseline from a different sitting.[^42]

That file is linked from the top of the instructions every session loads. It works by being read at the start of the work, not by turning red at the end of it, and I have come to think that is the only enforcement an AI-first repository actually has. The machine doesn't remember last Tuesday. It remembers what it was handed this morning. So the ledger of how we fail has to be part of what it is handed, or it will fail the same way with the same confidence.

The same instinct settled a smaller question that turned out to matter to me more than I expected. The tools wanted to sign the commits. A trailer on each one, a footer on each pull request, generated with, co-authored by. I said no: "I don't like the idea of AI by default attributing itself to the work. I am the director, designer, human in the loop taste, and game dev expert - ai are just machines operating on my intent."[^43] Every commit goes out under my name and my signature; the attribution setting is switched off and the rule is written into every agent's instructions. The journal still records which session did what. That is the record, not credit.[^44]

#### Where it stands, honestly

Seven days in, the engine draws twenty thousand crowd agents at 120 frames a second locked to the monitor, and a hundred thousand at 83. That 83 is the first render number that measured the engine rather than the monitor. Everything before it was drawn in step with the display, so the frame rate I got back was the refresh rate no matter what the card was doing.[^45] The integrated game loop, crowd simulation and rendering together, holds eighty thousand agents at 71 frames a second with none dropped.[^46] The animated version, twenty thousand agents each with a 22-bone skeleton moving, a small rig and twenty thousand of them, measured 20.93 milliseconds a frame, 4.26 over the 16.67 that sixty frames a second allows.[^47] And when I finally profiled it instead of assuming, the overrun was not the animation. The animation's own cost was 5.29 milliseconds and fully accounted for. The frame with no crowd in it at all had drifted 3.34 milliseconds worse across eleven days and several merges, and nobody had measured it in between.[^48]

The renderer barely exists. The editor does not. Every one of those numbers was taken on a card four or five times faster than the one I am budgeting for, which is the largest open caveat in the project and is written down as a risk rather than hidden.[^50]

What carried a week of work across thirteen sessions and two dead ones was not the code.[^51] The code is fine. It was the documents... the ones the machine reads before it does anything, and the ones I reread to catch it drifting toward a fun game, or toward building things I only mentioned, or toward a green checkmark nobody looked at.

When I sat down to write this, the first thing I asked for was a measurement: how big is the conversation that produced all this? The raw logs came to 85.6 megabytes. The part that is actually me and the machine talking, with the tool output stripped, is 1.05 megabytes. One point two percent.[^49] That is the part I read. That is the part that was worth keeping.

If you try this, you don't need the graphics card or the crowd. Take the four small things. A handoff note that says what only the conversation knows. A journal whose only trigger is a changed belief. A log of the reversible decisions, counted. And the sentence: a mentioned feature is not a work order.

The machine will forget all of it tonight. Write it down anyway. That is the job.
